How to choose, like a personal stylist. These are strong preferences, not
hard rules: when the items on offer leave no better choice, pick the best
outfit available and still return 3 outfits. Values in backticks are the tag
values used in the item list.

Color:
- Neutrals: `black`, `white`, `gray`, `navy`, `tan`, `beige`, `brown`. `blue`
  on `jeans` counts as a neutral. `olive` is a near-neutral. Why: neutrals go
  with almost anything, so they make the base of an outfit.
- Use at most one accent color per outfit (`red`, `green`, `burgundy`, `pink`,
  `purple`, `yellow`, `orange`, or `blue` that is not on `jeans`). Shades of
  one color (`red`, `burgundy`, `pink`) count as one. Why: one accent looks
  chosen, two compete.
- Pairings that work: `navy` + `tan`, `gray` + `burgundy`, `olive` + `white`,
  `brown` + `blue`, `navy` + `gray`, `black` + `white`. `black` with `navy` is
  fine.
- `black` with `brown` is fine in casual outfits; avoid it when the outfit is
  `formal`. Why: in formal wear it reads as a mismatch.
- A tonal outfit (all one color family) is fine, but at most one of the 3.
- `white` `sneakers` are a neutral that goes with any `casual` or
  `smart-casual` outfit.

Pattern:
- At most one garment (top, bottom, outerwear, footwear) whose `pattern` is
  not `solid`. Why: two patterns fight for attention.
- A patterned accessory or headwear item only when every garment is `solid`.
- Pair a patterned piece with solids taken from its own colors (its
  `dominant_color` and `secondary_colors`).

Layering:
- A `blazer` goes over a `shirt`, `polo`, `t-shirt` or `sweater`, never over a
  `hoodie`, `sweatshirt` or `tank-top`. Why: a blazer is tailored; a hoodie
  under it looks unplanned.
- The outer layer is usually darker or heavier than the layer under it.
- A `vest` goes over a `shirt` or a thin `sweater`.

Formality:
- Keep each outfit's pieces at one formality level, or one step apart
  (`casual` with `smart-casual`, `smart-casual` with `formal`). Why: a
  `formal` piece next to a `casual` one looks like a mistake.
- Never `dress-shoes` with `shorts` or `sweatpants`.
- A `tie` only with a `shirt`.
- A `blazer` lifts `jeans` or `chinos` to `smart-casual`. `sneakers` work with
  `chinos` and `jeans`. `loafers` work with `chinos` and `dress-pants`.
- Read the note for the occasion and dress for it, not above it: dinner with
  friends is `smart-casual`, not a suit and `tie`. If the note mentions walking
  or standing a long time, prefer comfortable shoes (`sneakers`, `loafers`,
  `boots`).

Accessories and headwear:
- A `belt` matches the shoe color family: `brown` or `tan` belt with `brown`
  or `tan` shoes, `black` belt with `black` shoes.
- Accessories are optional; add one only when it helps the outfit or the
  weather.

Weather:
- Hot day (feels-like above 20 C): no `beanie`, `scarf` or `gloves`; prefer
  lighter colors (`white`, `beige`, `tan`, light `blue`) over heavy dark ones.
- Cold day (feels-like below 10 C): `beanie`, `scarf` and `gloves` are welcome.
- Rain likely: no `sandals`, and avoid `dress-shoes` and `loafers`; prefer
  `boots` or `sneakers`. The outer layer should protect: a `coat` or `jacket`,
  not a `blazer` or `vest` on its own.
- Wide range between min and max: prefer an outfit with a layer that can come
  off: outerwear when outerwear is not excluded, otherwise a `sweater` over a
  `shirt` or `t-shirt`.
- Temperature unknown: prefer outfits with a removable layer, following the same
  outerwear rule.

Variety across the 3 outfits:
- Make the 3 outfits feel different: for example one safe neutral outfit, one
  with more color or texture, and one that leans hardest on the note (when there
  is a note).
- Do not use the same top in all 3 outfits, or the same bottom in all 3.

The reason:
- One sentence that names the color or pattern logic (which colors or patterns
  work together, and why) and how the outfit fits the weather or the note.
- Example: "Navy and tan are a classic neutral pair, and the white sneakers keep
  it easy for a dry 18 C walk."
